## 1. Scope, sources and membership

### 1.1 Rule applied

A writer is included when a surviving work is represented in the *Clavis Patrum Graecorum* (CPG), or in a named Syriac authority, and the writer receives independent treatment in a standard patrology or Syriac-literature handbook. The open [Clavis Clavium](https://clavis.brepols.net/clacla/OA/Link.aspx?clavis=cpg&number=2100) interface was spot-checked before it began returning its high-demand “Turnaway” page; exact numbers not independently reopened are marked for Task 8. Syriac membership uses [A Guide to Syriac Authors](https://syriaca.org/persons/index.html), the [Digital Syriac Corpus](https://syriaccorpus.org), and Brock et al., *Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage*, as cited by Syriaca.org. `[verified: reuse]` means the edition and licence were already checked in `docs/research/sources/greek-fathers.md` or `oriental-fathers.md` and their verification files; it is not a fresh claim about a work's contents.

The 749 endpoint is provisional. It includes John of Damascus so Pedro can later choose a shorter period without losing research. Writers assigned to Part A by the straddler rule—Eusebius of Caesarea and Alexander of Alexandria—are not duplicated. Councils, named theological movements and their fragmentary teachers belong to Part D.

## 2. Tier 1 entries

The Spanish forms below follow ordinary Spanish patristic usage; titles remain descriptive pending a final house style. “Accepted” refers to present attribution of the named work, not reception of its teaching.

### 2.1 Aphrahat

**Names:** ܐܦܪܗܛ · Aphrahat, the Persian Sage · Afraates. **Dates/place/language:** born unknown, active c. 337–345; probably within the Sasanian Empire; office uncertain, later tradition calls him a bishop; Syriac. [The DSC work authority](https://syriaca.org/work/8501) identifies him as author; dates derive from the dated acrostic sequence `[verified: reuse: oriental-fathers.md §2.1]`.

He is the earliest substantial Syriac prose writer whose corpus survives under his name. His twenty-three *Demonstrations* are alphabetically arranged expositions written in two dated groups and a final piece. They address faith, love, fasting, prayer, covenant life, penitents, resurrection, pastoral questions and Jewish-Christian controversy; describing their subjects does not assign their arguments to a modern church.

| Works | Date | Authenticity / transmission | Edition and reusable text |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Taḥwiyāthā / Demonstrations / Demostraciones* 1–10 | 337 | accepted corpus; Syriac manuscript transmission | Parisot, *Patrologia Syriaca* 1–2 is the standard older edition; DSC CC BY has all 23. Gwynn, NPNF2 13 has eight; Hallock has 2 and 7 with jurisdiction caveats. `[verified: reuse]` |
| Demonstrations 11–22 | 344 | accepted | DSC CC BY; public-domain English is incomplete. |
| Demonstration 23, “On the Grape” | 345 | accepted; alphabetic sequence completed separately | DSC CC BY; own translation required for full bilingual coverage. |

**Dates and authenticity:** the corpus's internal Seleucid-era dates are stronger evidence than the later biographical traditions; birth, death and episcopal office should remain “unknown” rather than harmonised from hagiography. **Checklist:** `sources/oriental-fathers.md` §§2.1, 2.6, 2.13.

### 2.2 Ephrem

**Names:** ܐܦܪܝܡ · Ephrem the Syrian · Efrén de Siria. **Dates/place/language:** c. 306–373; Nisibis, then Edessa after 363; deacon and teacher; Syriac. The exact early biography is obscured by later vitae; the Syriaca catalogue distinguishes works and lives rather than collapsing them ([Ephrem authority example](https://syriaca.org/work/464)). `[verified: reuse: oriental-fathers.md §2.2]`

Ephrem composed metrical homilies, teaching hymns and prose commentaries. His genuine Syriac corpus must be separated from the large Greek, Latin and Armenian *Ephrem* corpora. That separation matters more than any single date: a work labelled “Efrén” is not safe until its language and catalogue attribution are named.

| Works | Date | Authenticity / transmission | Edition and reusable text |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hymns on Faith, Nativity, Unleavened Bread, Crucifixion, Church, Virginity; Nisibene Hymns | 4th c.; several groups after 363 | core Syriac collections; individual cycles and sequence need edition-level checking | Beck CSCO is the modern reference; DSC CC BY includes Nativity and Nisibene material. Lamy 1882–1902 is a PD Syriac fallback. `[verified: reuse]` |
| *Mēmrē* and prose commentaries, including Genesis and Exodus | 4th c. | genuine core with corpus-level attribution problems | CSCO/modern critical references; use DSC or PD Syriac editions where present. |
| *Commentary on the Diatessaron* | 4th c. | Syriac fragments and a fuller Armenian version; transmission is indirect | Chester Beatty Syriac witness and modern editions are references; no blanket reusable modern translation. `[memory: verify]` |
| Greek *Ephrem* corpus and Testament | 4th c. onward | mixed, much pseudonymous; the [Testament authority](https://syriaca.org/work/1387) records separate witnesses and editions | keep each work as pseudo-Ephrem unless a critical catalogue accepts it. |

**Dates and authenticity:** 373 is the stable endpoint; the later vitae elaborate his encounter with Basil and other episodes and are evidence for reception, not direct biography. **Checklist:** `sources/oriental-fathers.md` §§2.2, 2.12–2.13.

### 2.3 Athanasius of Alexandria

**Names:** Ἀθανάσιος Ἀλεξανδρείας · Athanasius of Alexandria · Atanasio de Alejandría. **Dates/office/language:** born c. 295–300; died 2 May 373; bishop of Alexandria from 328, with repeated exiles; Egypt; Greek, with early Coptic/Syriac versions. CPG author and work nodes are the membership authority; `CPG 2100` is one work, not his author number. `[verified: partial Clavis; dates memory, verify]`

His writings arise from apologetic, episcopal and conciliar controversies, biblical interpretation, ascetic biography and correspondence. They are sources for fourth-century debates but the site must present each opponent and occasion rather than make him a proxy for a later tradition.

| Works | Date | Authenticity / transmission | Edition and reusable text |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Against the Gentiles* and *On the Incarnation* | c. 318–335; chronology disputed | accepted; two recensions discussed in modern editions | *Athanasius Werke* is critical; PTA supplies new CC BY-SA text and manuscripts. NPNF2 4 English is PD. `[verified: reuse]` |
| *Orations against the Arians* I–III | 339–357, debated | I–III accepted; a fourth oration is not treated as part of the same work | Tetz/Savvidis AW; First1KGreek; NPNF2 4. |
| *De decretis*, *De synodis*, *Apologia contra Arianos*, Festal Letters | 350s–367 | accepted; Festal Letter 39 survives fully in Coptic and partly in Greek/Syriac | PTA/First1KGreek and NPNF2 4; cite the language actually used. |
| *Life of Antony* | c. 356–362 | traditionally Athanasian and still commonly accepted; authorship and redaction have been debated | Bartelink SC is modern; PG/NPNF2 4 PD route, with attribution note. `[memory: verify debate]` |

**Dates and authenticity:** precise dating of the apologetic pair and orations is disputed; use ranges. The pseudo-Athanasian corpus (creed, questions, homilies) must not be imported under his name without its own CPG verdict. **Checklist:** `sources/greek-fathers.md` §§2, 5 row 19.

### 2.4 Cyril of Jerusalem

**Names:** Κύριλλος Ἱεροσολύμων · Cyril of Jerusalem · Cirilo de Jerusalén. **Dates/office/language:** c. 313–386; bishop of Jerusalem from c. 350, interrupted by exile; Palestine; Greek. `[memory: dates; CPG/edition verify]`

His catechetical corpus records instruction associated with baptismal preparation and the post-baptismal rites at Jerusalem. It is valuable as a situated fourth-century teaching cycle, not a universal transcript of all churches.

**Dates and authenticity:** show the two units separately and never hide the Mystagogical attribution dispute. **Checklist:** `sources/greek-fathers.md` §5 row 21.

### 2.11 Theodore of Mopsuestia

His biblical commentaries and catechetical homilies are central witnesses to Antiochene exegesis and initiation. Condemnation after his death led to loss in Greek and survival through translations and hostile excerpts; those are distinct evidence layers.
