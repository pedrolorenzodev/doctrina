### Cross-tradition baseline

Across these sources, the stable common ground is modest but clear:

- the middle recension contains seven letters written within the literary setting of a guarded journey toward death in Rome;
- five church letters respond to communities or delegates known to Ignatius, Romans asks an otherwise unvisited church not to obstruct his death, and Polycarp addresses an individual pastor;
- unity, resistance to false teaching, Christ's real embodied life and suffering, and the meaning of martyrdom recur across the corpus;
- Romans is unusually concentrated on the writer's coming death; Polycarp is unusually practical and pastoral; Trallians and Smyrnaeans contain the sharpest anti-docetic passages.

The sources diverge chiefly when they turn lines into evidence for a modern communion: Catholics emphasize Roman presidency, episcopal authority, “Catholic Church,” and Eucharistic real presence; Orthodox teachers emphasize episcopal-Eucharistic unity and reconciliation; Lutherans use Smyrnaeans for the true body in the Supper but reject Catholic reservation; Reformed and Baptist teachers emphasize anti-docetic Christology, apostolic continuity, martyrdom, and the difference between Ignatius's polity and later claims. None of those modern verdicts belongs in a neutral description of a letter.

## 1. Ephesians

**Sources by tradition.** Catholic: C1, C2; Orthodox: O1–O2; evangelical: E1; Reformed: R1–R2; Lutheran: L1 has no letter-specific description; Baptist: B1 treats it within the five community letters. C1 and O1 foreground obedience to the bishop; R2 foregrounds resistance to false teaching and the real incarnation. All agree that the letter is pastoral exhortation, not a treatise proving one modern polity.

**Shared points.** Written from Smyrna to the church at Ephesus after its delegation, led by Onesimus, met Ignatius. It thanks the visitors, urges concord, warns against false teaching, insists on Christ's real birth and passion, and uses musical and eucharistic images for unity.

**Divergences.** Catholic and Orthodox sources often make the bishop and Eucharist the interpretive center. Reformed teachers accept the unity and anti-heresy material but dispute reading Ignatius's local language as proof of later Roman Catholic structures. Those conclusions are excluded from the draft.

**Check against Lake 1912.** Salutation; Eph. 1–6, 7–9, 18–21, pp. 172–195. The delegation and Onesimus are in Eph. 1–2; the choir image is Eph. 4.2; false teachers appear in Eph. 7–9; embodied birth and passion in Eph. 18–19; “medicine of immortality” in Eph. 20.2.

**Draft Spanish description.** Desde Esmirna, Ignacio agradece a la iglesia de Éfeso la visita de Onesimo y su delegación, y la anima a mantenerse unida frente a enseñanzas extrañas, con la vida, muerte y resurrección reales de Jesús en el centro. La imagina como un coro: «unidos en concordia, reciban al unísono el tono de Dios y canten con una sola voz al Padre por medio de Jesucristo» (Efesios 4.2; traducción propia desde Lake 1912).

## 2. Magnesians

**Sources by tradition.** Catholic: C2 (corpus-level); Orthodox: O2; evangelical: E1; Reformed: R1–R2; Lutheran: no letter-specific treatment found in L1; Baptist: B1. The accessible popular sources rarely describe Magnesians separately; E1 and B1 supply the wider concerns of unity, conflict, and false teaching without turning the letter into a modern prooftext.

**Shared points.** Written from Smyrna to Magnesia after a delegation including the young bishop Damas. It urges the community not to despise his youth, seeks concord among its ministers and members, and contrasts life shaped by Jesus Christ with “Judaizing.”

**Divergences.** Modern teachers disagree over what Ignatius's polemic tells us about the separation of Judaism and Christianity and over what his threefold ministry can prove. The draft reports the polemic as his, without endorsing it or resolving those historical questions.

**Check against Lake 1912.** Salutation; Mag. 2–10, 13–15, pp. 196–211. Damas and the delegation are in Mag. 2–3; ministerial concord in Mag. 6–7 and 13; Sabbath/Lord's Day and “Judaizing” in Mag. 8–10; the closing greetings locate the letter at Smyrna.

**Draft Spanish description.** Desde Esmirna, Ignacio escribe a la iglesia de Magnesia después de recibir a una delegación encabezada por el joven obispo Damas: pide que su edad no sea motivo de menosprecio y llama a toda la comunidad a vivir en concordia. En su polémica contra quienes mezclaban prácticas, condensa el contraste así: «Es absurdo hablar de Jesucristo y practicar el judaísmo» (Magnesios 10.3; traducción propia desde Lake 1912).

## 3. Trallians

**Sources by tradition.** Catholic: C2; Orthodox: O2; evangelical: E1; Reformed: R2 and the linked Ligonier discussion specifically name Trallians as anti-docetic; Lutheran: no letter-specific treatment found in L1; Baptist: B1. The strongest convergence is its insistence that Jesus's suffering was real rather than apparent.

**Shared points.** Written from Smyrna after Polybius, bishop of Tralles, visited Ignatius on the church's behalf. It thanks the community, urges ordered unity, warns against alien teaching, and insists that Jesus was truly born, ate, drank, suffered, died, and rose.

**Divergences.** Traditions differ over the authority implied by bishop, presbyters, and deacons. They do not differ over the letter's explicit rejection of an unreal or merely apparent passion.

**Check against Lake 1912.** Salutation; Trall. 1–3, 6–10, 12–13, pp. 212–223. Polybius appears in Trall. 1; ministry in 2–3; warnings in 6–8; the compact anti-docetic confession in 9–10; the Smyrna location in 12.1.

**Draft Spanish description.** Desde Esmirna, Ignacio agradece a la iglesia de Tralles la visita de su obispo Polibio y la previene contra enseñanzas que convertían la vida y la pasión de Jesús en una mera apariencia. Su resumen no deja lugar a esa lectura: Jesús «nació verdaderamente, comió y bebió, fue perseguido verdaderamente bajo Poncio Pilato, fue crucificado verdaderamente y murió» (Tralianos 9.1; traducción propia desde Lake 1912).

## 4. Romans

**Sources by tradition.** Catholic: C1 reads the salutation and Rom. 3–4 in relation to Roman authority; Orthodox: O2 frames the death as participation in Christ's triumph; evangelical: E1; Reformed: R1 treats martyrdom; Lutheran: no letter-specific treatment found in L1; Baptist: B1 makes Romans the center of its study. All agree that this letter differs from the other church letters because it asks an unvisited community not to intervene and is overwhelmingly about the approaching death.

**Shared points.** Written from Smyrna to the Roman church without reference to a delegation from Rome. Ignatius asks its Christians not to use influence to prevent his expected execution, requests prayer, and describes martyrdom as the point at which his discipleship will be tested and completed.

**Divergences.** Catholic apologetics reads “presiding in love,” Peter and Paul, and Roman teaching as evidence of Roman primacy. Other sources focus on martyrdom or dispute that inference. Modern readers also disagree sharply over whether his desire for death is admirable witness, dangerous excess, or rhetoric shaped by the situation.

**Check against Lake 1912.** Salutation; Rom. 1–10, pp. 224–238. The request not to intervene runs through Rom. 1–8; the wheat image is Rom. 4.1; prayer for strength and for Antioch appears in Rom. 3, 9–10; Rom. 10.3 dates the letter to 24 August in Lake.

**Draft Spanish description.** Desde Esmirna, Ignacio escribe a una iglesia de Roma que no había enviado delegados para pedirle que no impida su ejecución y que ore para que él sostenga hasta el final lo que dice desear. La imagen que domina la carta es extrema e inolvidable: «Soy trigo de Dios y los dientes de las fieras me muelen, para que sea hallado pan puro de Cristo» (Romanos 4.1; traducción propia desde Lake 1912).

## 5. Philadelphians

**Sources by tradition.** Catholic: C2; Orthodox: O2; evangelical: E1 specifically notes the Judaizers and the disputed “archives” passage; Reformed: R1; Lutheran: no letter-specific treatment found in L1; Baptist: B1 places it among the five community letters. Teachers agree about a conflict over unity and Judaism, but not about the precise opponents or the force of “archives.”

**Shared points.** Written from Troas to a church Ignatius had visited. It recalls a dispute, urges unity around one assembly, one Eucharist, and its ministers, argues that Christ's cross and resurrection are the decisive “charters,” and asks the church to send a deacon to rejoice over restored peace in Antioch.

**Divergences.** Scholars dispute the punctuation and referent of “archives,” and modern traditions draw different conclusions from the single Eucharist and bishop. The draft keeps the letter's own striking Christ-centered formulation without defining its opponents more precisely than the text permits.

**Check against Lake 1912.** Salutation; Philad. 2–8, 10–11, pp. 238–250. One Eucharist/altar/bishop is Philad. 4.1; dispute and “charters” are 6–8; the request for a deacon and news of Antioch are 10–11; the Troas location is 11.2. Lake explicitly notes the punctuation ambiguity at 8.2.

**Draft Spanish description.** Desde Troas, Ignacio escribe a la iglesia de Filadelfia después de una visita marcada por disputas: pide una sola reunión en torno a Jesús y solicita que envíen un diácono a celebrar la paz recuperada en Antioquía. Frente a quienes exigían documentos como criterio, responde: «Para mí, los archivos son Jesucristo; el archivo inviolable es su cruz, su muerte y su resurrección» (Filadelfios 8.2; traducción propia desde Lake 1912).

## 6. Smyrnaeans

**Sources by tradition.** Catholic: C1 makes this its principal source for “Catholic Church,” bishop, and Eucharistic real presence; Orthodox: O1–O2 emphasize unity and resistance to heresy; evangelical/Reformed: R2 and its linked Ligonier article identify Smyrnaeans as a central anti-docetic letter; Lutheran: L1 quotes Smyrn. 6 for Christ's true body in the Supper while rejecting Catholic reservation; Baptist: B1 connects its Christology to Ignatius's account of martyrdom.

**Shared points.** Written from Troas to the church that had recently hosted Ignatius. It vigorously insists on Christ's real flesh, suffering, and bodily resurrection, connects communal and Eucharistic order with the bishop, reports peace in Antioch, and asks Smyrna to send an envoy.

**Divergences.** Catholic, Orthodox, and Lutheran teachers all take its Eucharistic language seriously but disagree over what follows for later sacramental practice and authority; Reformed teachers prioritize its anti-docetic Christology and dispute Catholic institutional conclusions. The draft therefore centers the point no tradition in the sample disputes: the embodied resurrection.

**Check against Lake 1912.** Salutation; Smyrn. 1–8, 11–12, pp. 250–266. Christ's real flesh and resurrection dominate 1–5; Eucharist and communal order appear in 6–8; “Catholic Church” is 8.2; Antioch and the envoy are 11–12; Troas is named in 12.1.

**Draft Spanish description.** Desde Troas, Ignacio escribe a la iglesia de Esmirna para agradecer su hospitalidad, combatir la idea de que Jesús solo parecía tener cuerpo y pedir un enviado que celebre la paz de Antioquía. Lo afirma de forma directa: «Sé y creo que estaba en la carne incluso después de la resurrección» (Esmirniotas 3.1; traducción propia desde Lake 1912).

## 7. Polycarp

**Sources by tradition.** Catholic: C2; Orthodox: O1 quotes its military imagery and O2 supplies a corpus introduction; evangelical: E1; Reformed: R1; Lutheran: no letter-specific treatment found in L1; Baptist: B1 identifies it as the sole letter to an individual and as mostly pastoral exhortation from one bishop to another.

**Shared points.** Written from Troas to Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, with counsel that broadens to the whole community. It treats endurance, attention to individuals, widows, slaves, marriage, church officers, and the practical problem of appointing a messenger to circulate the news from Antioch.

**Divergences.** Teachers vary in how strongly they read the latter part as addressed through Polycarp to the congregation, and in what its ministry language proves. Its practical, personal character is common ground.

**Check against Lake 1912.** Salutation; Poly. 1–8, pp. 266–277. Personal pastoral counsel fills 1–3; widows, slaves, marriage, and communal duties are in 4–6; the Antioch messenger is 7–8; the sudden departure from Troas is 8.1.

**Draft Spanish description.** Desde Troas, Ignacio le escribe a Policarpo de Esmirna una breve guía pastoral: atender a cada persona, proteger a las viudas, tratar con cuidado la esclavitud y el matrimonio, y sostener a la comunidad mientras organiza un mensajero para Antioquía. Su consejo más visual es también el más breve: «Mantente firme como un yunque golpeado» (A Policarpo 3.1; traducción propia desde Lake 1912).

## Delivery recommendation

Use the seven draft descriptions as short Screen 09 copy only after Pedro reviews the Spanish translations. Keep the source-by-tradition and divergence material in research, not in the UI. In particular, do not turn Ignatius into a Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Reformed, evangelical, or Baptist witness: the modern sources illuminate why readers select different passages; they do not own him.
