# 06 · The quotation sign: history, the diple, neutrality

Accessed 2026-11-04. Round 1 already covered: guillemets first printed 1527 (03 §6), Origen's asterisk/obelus (03 §6), the codex and Hexapla columns (03 §6). This file goes deeper on the diple and on what the shapes actually look like.

## 1. The short answer

1. **The diple (`>`) is the documented ancestor of quotation marks and, in Christian manuscripts, specifically the sign for «this is Scripture being quoted».** Isidore of Seville (early 7th c.) says so in one line (§2.1); 4th-century Greek Bibles (Vaticanus, Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus) and a c. 200 papyrus of Irenaeus use it that way (§2.2–2.3); Ruth Finnegan's history of quotation traces the line diple → marginal commas → inverted commas and guillemets (§2.4). [verified]
2. **It predates every modern tradition** and is a scribal device, not a devotional symbol. I found no church body that uses a diple or a guillemet as its emblem (§5). Neutrality: shared and bookish [inference from §2, §5].
3. **What the scribes drew is not what fonts draw.** The diple in Codex Vaticanus is a small, asymmetric pen mark with a short light upper stroke and a heavier, steeper lower stroke, often with a tiny hook at the start; at a glance it looks like a numeral «7» [verified, visual, §3]. Newsreader's guillemet, by contrast, is a straight-sided, constant-width geometric chevron with a flat-cut tip, the same construction as a UI arrow [verified, computed, §4]. That is one concrete reason D3 can read as «interface icon» rather than «text» [inference].
4. **Spanish uses « » first** in print (RAE), and they sit centred on the x-height, not raised like English quotes (§6). French spaces them; German points them inward (»…«).

## 2. History, with sources

### 2.1 Isidore of Seville, *Etymologiae* I.21 «De notis sententiarum»

LacusCurtius edition (Lindsay's Latin text), https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Isidore/1*.html [verified]:

- I.21.13: «﹥ Diple. Hanc scriptores nostri adponunt in libris ecclesiasticorum virorum ad separanda vel ad demonstranda testimonia sanctarum Scripturarum.» My translation: «Diple. Our copyists put this in the books of churchmen to set apart or to point out the quotations [*testimonia*] of the Holy Scriptures.» (Wikipedia's «Quotation mark» gives «Our copyists place this sign in the books of the people of the Church», https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotation_mark [verified].)
- I.21.15: «⸖ Diple περιεστιγμένη, id est cum geminis punctis. Hanc antiqui in his opponebant quae Zenodotus Ephesius non recte adiecerat, aut detraxerat, aut permutaverat. In his et nostri ea usi sunt.» My translation: «Diple *periestigmene*, i.e. with two dots. The ancients put it against what Zenodotus of Ephesus had wrongly added, removed or changed. Ours used it for those too.»
- I.21.16: «Diple ὀβολισμένη interponitur ad separandos in comoediis vel tragoediis periodos» (diple with obelus: separates periods in comedies and tragedies).
- The same list of 26 notae includes the asterisk, obelus, lemniscus, antisigma, *ceraunium* and, at I.21.22, «☧ Chrisimon. Haec sola ex voluntate uniuscuiusque ad aliquid notandum ponitur» (the chi-rho as a free-use reader's mark). [verified] — a reminder that in late antiquity the chi-rho was also a margin sign, but round 1 already discarded it (05 §3).

### 2.2 Greek Bibles of the 4th–5th c. and later minuscules

- **Gregory R. Lanier**, «Scribal Treatment of Extra-Biblical Citations in the New Testament», *Reformed Faith and Practice* 10.1 (Spring 2026), https://journal.rts.edu/?p=2774 [verified]: defines the device as «a diple (> or <) in the margin to the left or right (respectively) of a line containing cited text»; lists the manuscripts that use diplae «at least sometimes to mark cited material»: **GA 01 (Sinaiticus), 02 (Alexandrinus), 03 (Vaticanus), 012, 1, 1424, 1739**; «often the diplae appear to be applied by the original scribe(s)»; «such marginal markings pre-date NT scribes and were used in secular works to mark cited material»; GA 1739 «sometimes uses an obelus (÷) in the margin instead of a diple»; in GA 03 «the diplae were not always reinked». Patristic papyri also mark both OT and NT citations (his n. 15: P.Oxy. 405 and P.Mich. 764), hence the scholarly label «diplae sacrae». He cites Charles E. Hill: the diplae show «not only a distinct awareness of citation, but an awareness that what was marked was being set apart as scripture» (Hill 2012, in *The Early Text of the New Testament*, OUP, 280).
- **P.Oxy. 405** is a fragment of Irenaeus of Lyon's *Against Heresies* (written c. 180), copied c. 200, containing a quotation of Matthew 3:16–17 (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papyrus_Oxyrhynchus_405 [verified]; that page does not mention the diple — the diple claim rests on Lanier's footnote).
- **Charles E. Hill**, «The New Testament Citing the New Testament in (Copies of) the New Testament: Diples and Testimony Lists in Early Manuscripts», *TC* 29 (2024) 45–70 (bibliographic record, https://www.aiep-iaps.org/new-testament-citing-new-testament-copies-new-testament-diples-and-testimony-lists-early [verified]). The PDF (https://jbtc.org/v29/TC_Hill.pdf) returned HTTP 522 twice this run. From search summaries only [snippet]: the four great pandects (01, 02, 03, 04) sometimes put diples in the left margin at OT citations; no scribe marked all of them, Vaticanus's came closest; some diples mark NT texts cited in the NT (Acts 13:25, 1 Tim 5:18b, 2 Pet 1:17).
- **Vaticanus details** (Philip B. Payne in a 2010 blog series hosted by *Evangelical Textual Criticism*, https://evangelicaltextualcriticism.blogspot.com/2010/01/putting-distigmai-in-their-place-payne_19.html [verified]): «there are 123 isolated diple or sets of diple on contiguous lines in the Vaticanus NT»; «the same scribe who wrote the text also wrote most of the diple»; some later ones «look like a greater-than sign and lack[s] the calligraphic quality of the original diple»; at p. 1491 some were «supplemented with larger diple lacking the small upper hook»; «In one instance, 1455 C 30, a diple points backwards.» Ulrich Schmid (comment): «The diples to mark OT quotations in Vaticanus is a remarkably consistent feature.» (Blog, not peer-reviewed; counts are Payne's.)

### 2.3 Latin manuscripts

From Finnegan (below), who cites M. B. Parkes, *Pause and Effect* (1992) [verified as Finnegan's report; Parkes not opened]:
- A 6th-century manuscript of **Hilary of Poitiers**, *De Trinitate*: the Scripture lines are introduced by «he said» or «what has been written» with «an inward-pointing arrowhead in the left hand margin» (Parkes 1992: 168–9, pl. 5).
- An 8th-century manuscript of **Bede**'s commentary on Proverbs (MS Bodley 819, fol. 16): marks «more like elongated commas than arrowheads», repeated beside each line, «large at its start, small in continuing line(s)».
- Later forms: «dots (single, double, multiple), … squiggles, curves, … r-shapes, V's or Y's with or without dots inside», and a 6th-century insular manuscript with «a tiny flourish (7-shaped or like a bird in flight) to the left of each line» (Lowe 1972: 4, quoted by Finnegan).
- **Codex Bezae** (5th c.) marks OT quotations by **indenting**, not by diples (Finnegan; a search summary of Hill says the same [snippet]).

### 2.4 From the diple to inverted commas and guillemets

- **Ruth Finnegan**, *Why Do We Quote? The Culture and History of Quotation*, Open Book Publishers 2011, ch. 4, https://books.openedition.org/obp/921 [verified]:
  - «The answer would seem to lie in a little graphic sign used by ancient Greek editors to draw attention to something noteworthy in the text. … Shaped like an arrowhead, it was known as the diplē ('double') from the two lines that formed its wedge shape.»
  - «For mediaeval Christian writers and readers their prime purpose was to signal scriptural citations, later extended to the writings of the patristic 'fathers of the church'.»
  - «By the early sixteenth century however comma-shape quotation marks, successors of the diple, were being cut in metal type»; by the 17th century «mostly as doubled semicircular signs, now becoming known as 'inverted' or 'turned' commas»; at first «set in the margin outside the line», brought «within the print measure» towards the end of the 16th century.
  - «the diple-based forms remain the most visible … and in their continental chevron versions (« ») even more directly allied to the original arrowhead than in the curved inverted commas of English-language usage.»
  - The guillemet name: «'Little Willy', after the sixteenth-century typecutter Guillaume [William] Le Bé».
- **Aristarchus** (2nd c. BC Homeric scholar): the diple «marked lines whose language or content was perhaps also exegetically noteworthy» and «pointed to a corresponding explanation in a commentary»; the dotted diple marked where Aristarchus's text differed from Zenodotus's (Wikipedia, «Aristarchian symbols», https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristarchian_symbols [verified]; its scholarly sources: Schironi, *GRBS* 57, 2017; McNamee 1992). The signs survive in the 10th-century Venetus A of the *Iliad* [snippet; also Finnegan, verified].
- **Origen's Hexapla** used Aristarchus's **asterisk, obelus and metobelus** in the Septuagint column (Catholic Encyclopedia, J. F. Fenlon, 1910, https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07316a.htm [verified]: «he adopted (and placed in the Septuagint column only) the symbols used by Aristarchus in his edition of Homer»). **No source read this run says Origen used the diple** in the Hexapla; the brief's phrase «diple in Origen's Hexapla apparatus» could not be confirmed. Use Origen only for the asterisk/obelus story.
- **1527, the first printed guillemets.** TLFi (CNRTL), https://www.cnrtl.fr/etymologie/guillemet [verified]: «Le signe apparaît pour la 1re fois dès 1527 chez Alde et J. Bade ds Priscien»; the word «Prob. dimin. de Guillaume», «inventeur présumé de ce signe»; word attested 1677. French Wikipedia (https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guillemet [verified]) repeats it and says the invention was «faussement attribuée à l'imprimeur Guillaume en 1622». English Wikipedia ties the name to Guillaume Le Bé (1525–1598) «apparently» and says he «did not invent the symbols» (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guillemet [verified]). Wikimedia Commons holds a crop from that 1527 Priscian with the marginal sign: two small stacked commas, not chevrons (https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Diple_in_Prisciani_grammatici_Caesariensis_Libri_omnes_(1527).png, public domain [verified, visual]; copy in `refs/manuscripts/priscian-1527-diple.png`). **Correction to round 1** (03 §6 says «printed by Josse Bade»): TLFi credits **Aldus and J. Bade** (two presses) for 1527.
- **Shape of print guillemets in the 20th c.:** Imprimerie nationale's Didot guillemets, 1934 (Commons, public domain [verified, visual], `refs/manuscripts/guillemets-imprimerie-nationale-1934.png`): small, heavy, wedge-shaped (thick outer edge, thin inner), sitting mid-height.

## 3. What the diple actually looks like (Codex Vaticanus, looked at this run)

Images: Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, Vat.gr.1209, IIIF, https://digi.vatlib.it/view/MSS_Vat.gr.1209 (manifest https://digi.vatlib.it/iiif/MSS_Vat.gr.1209/manifest.json). Copyright BAV, «all rights reserved» watermark; crops kept in `refs/manuscripts/` for internal study only.

| Page | Text | What I see [verified, visual] |
|---|---|---|
| p. 1311 col. A (Luke 4:17–19, Isaiah quoted) | `vaticanus-p1311-luke4-diples.jpg`, `-diples-zoom.jpg`, `-single-diple-x10.jpg` | one mark per line of the quotation, in faded ochre ink, in the left margin about one letter-width from the column; each mark is roughly half the cap height of the text |
| p. 1491 col. C (Gal 4:27, Isaiah quoted) | `vaticanus-p1491-gal4-diples-zoom.jpg`, `-single-diple-x10.jpg` | same, darker; these are the ones Payne says were «supplemented»; shapes vary slightly line to line |

The shape, described only from what is visible:
- Two strokes meeting at a vertex that points **right** (towards the text), i.e. `>`.
- **Asymmetric.** The upper stroke is short, light, and close to horizontal (roughly 15–25° below horizontal, by eye). The lower stroke is longer, heavier, and much steeper (roughly 55–65°, by eye), running down and to the left. The opening between the arms is therefore not symmetric about a horizontal axis: the whole sign looks rotated clockwise compared with a typeset `>`.
- A small **blob or hook** at the start of the upper stroke (Payne's «small upper hook»). With the asymmetry this makes many of them read as a small «7» or a «ɀ»-like squiggle.
- Ink-pen modulation: thicker where the pen pressed (the lower stroke), thin entries and exits. No two are identical.
- No dots; these are plain diples, not *periestigmene*.

**None of the site's fonts (Newsreader, Source Serif 4, Gentium Book Plus Regular/Bold) contains U+2E16** [verified, computed: cmap check]; the column labelled «Gentium U+2E16» in `refs/manuscripts/glyph-comparison.png` is therefore a macOS fallback font's glyph (a geometric `>` with one dot above and one below the vertex), not Gentium's. The Commons drawing of the *diple periestigmene* (`diple-periestigmene-commons.png`, CC BY-SA 3.0) shows three variants with dots to the right of or between the arms [verified, visual]. Manuscript placement of the dots varies (not checked in a manuscript this run).

- Each chevron is a **constant-width straight bar bent at ~84°** (arms ≈ 42–43° from horizontal), with a short vertical flat at the tip (≈ 88 units at wght 800) and slanted cut ends. At display sizes the cut ends make the arms look slightly wedge-shaped, but there is no calligraphic stress and no curve [verified, computed + visual].
- Newsreader's `>` is drawn the same way (8 points, straight).
- The guillemet sits roughly on the x-height band (x-height 852 units; « spans y ≈ 30–880), consistent with Spanish «centradas» (§6).
- For comparison (rendered this run, `refs/manuscripts/glyph-comparison.png`): **Gentium Book Plus Bold** draws « with curves and flared, leaf-like arms (more pen-like); **Source Serif 4 Black** draws a wedge (heavy outside, thin at the point); **Newsreader Italic 800** is a slanted straight chevron.

Consequence [inference]: D3 is in effect «two straight chevrons on a tile». Straight constant-width chevrons are the vocabulary of UI arrows (back, rewind, collapse; see 07). A pen-built or wedge-built quote sign, or the asymmetric manuscript diple, would read more as **writing** than as **interface**.

## 5. Neutrality and tradition use

- **Pre-division and scribal.** Aristarchus (pagan, 2nd c. BC) → Greek Christian scribes (c. 200 onwards) → Latin scribes (6th–8th c.) → printers (1527). The 4th-century codices that carry it are shared textual witnesses for every tradition's Bible scholarship [inference from §2]. Codex Vaticanus is held by the Vatican Library; the sign itself is not a Catholic emblem [inference].
- **Fathers, without assigning them.** Irenaeus (P.Oxy. 405), Hilary of Poitiers and Bede appear here only as authors whose manuscripts carry the sign (§2.2–2.3). Isidore is the 7th-century source that names it. None is assigned to a modern tradition.
- **Church emblems built from quotation marks or the diple:** none found. Round 1's 36 Christian favicons contain no typographic sign (02 §4 «open territory»); one broad search for Christian logos using quotation marks or guillemets returned only generic logo-contest pages, no church body or registered emblem [snippet, negative result]. **Not checked:** WIPO / EUIPO trademark databases (would need a real search before adoption).
- **Typographic, not confessional:** Spanish, French, Italian, Greek, Russian and others use guillemets as ordinary quotation marks (§6); no tradition avoids them [inference; I found no source saying any church avoids them].
- Verdict: **neutral, shared, bookish; with a specifically early-Christian use (marking Scripture quoted) that every tradition inherits.** Name-independent.

## 6. Conventions today

- **Spanish (RAE, *Diccionario panhispánico de dudas*, «comillas»**, https://www.rae.es/dpd/comillas, read via Playwright [verified]): «las comillas angulares, también llamadas latinas o españolas (« »)»; «las angulares se escriben centradas»; «En los textos impresos, se recomienda utilizar en primera instancia las comillas angulares». Nesting: « “ ‘ ’ ” ».
- **French:** outward « », spaced by narrow no-break spaces, «except no spaces in Switzerland» (English Wikipedia, «Guillemet» [verified]).
- **German:** inward »…« in books; „…“ in newspapers and handwriting (Wikipedia [verified]; Finnegan n. 5 [verified]). Duden's rule page shows „…“ and says other forms are common in typesetting, without naming »…« (https://www.duden.de/sprachwissen/rechtschreibregeln/anfuehrungszeichen [verified]). **So a lone « reads «opening quote» in Spanish/French and «closing quote» in German.**
- **Spanish publishing vs daily use:** Wikipedia lists Spanish guillemets as «uncommon in daily usage, but commonly used in publishing» [verified].
- **Unicode** (NamesList 18.0.0, https://www.unicode.org/Public/UCD/latest/ucd/NamesList.txt [verified]):
  - U+00AB LEFT-POINTING DOUBLE ANGLE QUOTATION MARK, «= left guillemet», «* usually opening, sometimes closing»; U+00BB right, «usually closing, sometimes opening».
  - U+2039 / U+203A single angle quotation marks.
  - U+2E16 DOTTED RIGHT-POINTING ANGLE «= diple periestigmene», in the block «Ancient Greek textual symbols» (U+2E0E–2E16, with the paragraphos, dotted obelos, ancoras).
  - **There is no dedicated code point for the plain diple**; `>` (U+003E) is used, and the names list cross-references `>` to › (U+203A).
  - U+276E/276F HEAVY … ANGLE QUOTATION MARK ORNAMENT exist as dingbats.

## 7. What this means for the mark [inference]

1. H3 holds in substance: **the diple is the better-grounded sign**, and it is the literal ancestor of the guillemet. It also has the exact meaning the site wants («this is Scripture / a source being quoted, not our words»).
2. But a typeset `>` alone is «greater than» / «next» / «play» (see 07). The grounding only shows if the drawing carries the **manuscript features**: asymmetry, the steeper heavy lower arm, a hook or pen entry, margin placement beside «lines». Without them it is just a chevron.
3. A single `>` points **towards the text** in the manuscripts (the margin is on the left). In a tab or header the text is to the right of the mark, so `>` (or a diple drawn pointing right) keeps that relation; « points away from the content that follows. [inference from §3 + layout]
4. The dotted *diple periestigmene* is well attested (Isidore I.21.15, Unicode U+2E16) and the dots make it non-UI; but its historical meaning is «edition differs», not «quotation». Usable as form, with that caveat stated honestly.
5. Hill and Lanier's «set apart as scripture» is the strongest one-line story: *the sign scribes used to set Scripture apart from the commentator's own words* — which is the site's rule (each tradition in its own words; the site never concludes).
