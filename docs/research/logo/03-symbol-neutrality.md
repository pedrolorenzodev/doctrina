# 03 · Symbol vocabulary and neutrality

Accessed 2026-11-04. Tags as in README. Wikipedia extracts were read through the MediaWiki API (`action=query&prop=extracts`) and are cited as «Wikipedia, <article>»; Wikipedia is a secondary source — where a scholar or institution was reachable it is cited instead or as well. Britannica and Shady Characters (Keith Houston) blocked the fetcher this run (403) and are not cited.

Neutrality verdicts:
- **shared** — attested before the 1054 / Reformation divisions and used today across Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant bodies without being any one body's emblem;
- **tradition-owned** — the recognised emblem of one tradition or body (may be a registered mark);
- **contested** — rejected or avoided by at least one tradition, or carrying a strong non-Christian association;
- **secular-bookish** — a sign of writing, scholarship or the book, with no denominational load.

Rule from AGENTS.md and PROJECT.md: no symbol owned by a tradition may be shown as Doctrina's neutral default; Church Fathers (and their symbols) are not assigned to a modern tradition. Note: PROJECT.md lists six MVP traditions (Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Reformed, Baptist, **Pentecostal**), not five.

## 1. Christograms and Greek letters

**Chi-Rho ☧.** «One of the earliest forms of the Christogram», chi and rho of ΧΡΙΣΤΟΣ superimposed; on Constantine's standard, the Labarum (312); pre-Christian use as a marginal mark abbreviating *chrēston* («good») and on coins of Ptolemy III; appears on some pre-Constantinian sarcophagi (Wikipedia, Chi Rho [verified]). Today used across Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican and Protestant liturgical art [memory — no survey found; do not quote as fact]. Risks: the Constantinian/imperial-military reading; as a favicon it is a recognisable «Christian brand» symbol. **Verdict: shared**, but strongly religious and imperial in connotation; heavily used (vestments, church logos) [memory].

**Staurogram ⳨ (tau-rho).** A rho superimposed on a tau; pre-Christian (Herod's coins, 37 BC); first Christian use «to abbreviate *stauros* … in very early New Testament manuscripts such as 𝔓66, 𝔓45 and 𝔓75» (Wikipedia, Staurogram [verified]). Larry Hurtado: «a device formed by superimposing the Greek capital-letter "rho" over the Greek capital-letter "tau"», «intended as a pictographic representation of the crucified Jesus»; «In all the earliest uses, the device appears in these texts as part of the way the Greek words» for cross and crucify are written; uses in «several NT manuscripts datable to ca. 200 CE» (L. Hurtado, «The "Staurogram"», 2010-07-12, https://larryhurtado.wordpress.com/2010/07/12/the-staurogram/ [verified]); his BAR digest «Earliest Depiction of Jesus' Crucifixion» (2013) (https://larryhurtado.wordpress.com/2013/02/15/the-staurogram-newly-published-article/ [verified]). Unicode U+2CE8 and U+101A0. **Verdict: shared** (manuscript-era, pre-division, owned by nobody), **scholarly-niche**: almost no lay recognition [inference], which makes it ownable but also means it reads as «a P with a bar» to most people. It is a cross-image in origin; Reformed readers who reject images of Christ (see §4) may object to a crucifixion pictogram — flag it [inference].

**Nomina sacra overline.** Contractions of divine names with an overline (ΙΣ, ΧΣ, ΘΣ, ΚΣ); 15 words in Metzger's list, «all found in Greek manuscripts of the 3rd century and earlier, except Mother»; practice tied to the early Christian codex; «an act of reverence rather than a purely practical space-saving device» (Wikipedia, Nomina sacra, citing Metzger and Hurtado [verified]). The overline survives as the *titlo* in Church Slavonic (same article). **Verdict: shared, secular-bookish in look** (a bar over letters). A strong, little-used scribal device: an overline is a «this is a sacred/abbreviated name» marker without a cross.

**IHS.** Abbreviation of ΙΗΣΟΥΣ; popularised by Bernardino of Siena on a blazing sun; «The IHS monogram with the H surmounted by a cross above three nails and surrounded by a Sun is the emblem of the Jesuits, according to tradition introduced by Ignatius of Loyola in 1541» (Wikipedia, Christogram [verified]). **Verdict: tradition-owned** (Western Catholic, specifically Jesuit in its sun form).

**IC XC.** «In Eastern Orthodoxy and Eastern Catholicism, the most widely used Christogram is a four-letter abbreviation, ΙϹ ΧϹ … written with titlo» (Wikipedia, Christogram [verified]); on the GOARCH seal surveyed in 02 (with NI KA). **Verdict: tradition-owned** (Eastern).

**IX monogram** (iota + chi, «Jesus Christ», on early ossuaries in Palestine; Irenaeus and Pachomius comment on it) (Wikipedia, Christian symbolism [verified]). **Verdict: shared, rare.** Visually an asterisk/six-spoke wheel — risk: generic.

**Ichthys / ΙΧΘΥΣ.** Acrostic «Jesus Christ, God's Son, Savior»; first Christian appearances 2nd century; Tertullian «We, little fishes…»; Augustine comments on it; catacombs of St Sebastian and Priscilla; today «colloquially as the "Jesus fish"… on vehicles, necklaces and laptop stickers» (Wikipedia, Ichthys [verified]). A 4th-century form superimposes the five letters into an eight-spoked wheel (same). **Verdict: shared but popular-evangelical in modern connotation** (bumper sticker); cliché for a study tool [inference]. The wheel form is obscure and could pass as abstract.

**Alpha and Omega Α Ω.** Rev 1:8, 21:6, 22:13; used in the catacombs; hung from cross arms in early art (Wikipedia, Alpha and Omega [verified]). Ephrem the Syrian describes a tau-rho with alpha and omega (Wikipedia, Staurogram [verified]). **Verdict: shared.** Two letters → harder at 16 px; also generic («beginning and end», used by many secular brands) [inference].

**Greek letters as initials (Δ, Λ, Θ).** No specific Christian ownership found. Λ is the initial of Λόγος (John 1:1) [memory: obvious etymology]; Δ of δόγμα / διδαχή / Δοκτρίνα [inference]. Risk: Δ is the generic «change/delta» sign (science, startups); Λ reads as lambda (Half-Life, functional programming, AWS Lambda) [memory]. **Verdict: secular**, name-dependent if Δ = «Doctrina».

**Word marks «Logos», «Didache», «Didaskalia».** «Logos» is already the brand of the biggest Bible software (Logos Bible Software / Faithlife, in 02) — collision. «Didache» is the title of a 1st–2nd-century church manual [memory] and of at least one Catholic study-Bible product [memory — not checked; check before use]. **Verdict: shared as words, crowded as brands.**

## 2. Crosses

**Christian cross / crucifix.** «A cross with a figure of Christ affixed to it is termed a crucifix»; the crucifix is used by Catholics and many Lutherans, while «Protestant denominations, and … the Assyrian Church of the East and Armenian Apostolic Church … prefer to use a cross without the figure» (Wikipedia, Crucifix [verified]). Reformed writers (Ridley, Calfhill, Beza) «rejected practices that they described as cross worship» (Wikipedia, Christian cross [verified]). **Verdict: the plain cross is the most broadly shared Christian sign, but it is the default of every church logo (02: 7 of 36 Christian favicons contain a cross: Logos, Biblia, USCCB, LCMS, ELCA, OrthodoxWiki, GOARCH) and DESIGN.md bans «crosses as decoration».** A cross as the brand mark of a site that never takes sides would read as «a Christian organisation», not «a study of Christian traditions» [inference].

**Greek cross (equal arms).** Generic heraldic form (Wikipedia, Christian cross [verified]). In a circle it becomes the **sun cross**: ADL lists the Celtic cross as «one of the most important and commonly used white supremacist symbols», and the short version «surrounded by a circle» as «more commonly used by white supremacists»; the traditional long-shafted Celtic cross «does not denote white supremacy or racism» absent other symbols (ADL, Hate Symbols, «Celtic Cross», https://www.adl.org/resources/hate-symbol/celtic-cross [verified]). **Verdict: contested in the circled form.** Any geometric mark that ends up as «+ in a circle» must be dropped (USCCB's favicon in 02 is exactly that shape).

**Celtic (high) cross.** Irish/Scottish monastic heritage; see ADL above. **Verdict: contested.**

**Orthodox three-bar cross.** Russian Orthodox cross (three bars, lower one slanted) used in Russia since the 16th century, related to Byzantine forms with a slanted footrest from the 6th century; «Today it is a symbol of the Russian Orthodox Church» (Wikipedia, Russian Orthodox cross [verified]). Byzantine-Rite Lutherans put it inside a Luther rose (Wikipedia, Luther rose [verified]). **Verdict: tradition-owned** (Russian/Slavic Orthodox; also national-political load).

**Patriarchal cross / Cross of Lorraine.** Two bars; Byzantine and heraldic; national emblems (Wikipedia, Patriarchal cross [verified]). **Verdict: contested/political** (national emblem, Free French) [memory for the Free French use].

**Jerusalem cross.** Cross potent with four Greek crosslets; coat of arms of the Crusader Kingdom after 1099; Order of the Holy Sepulchre; flag of Georgia; Episcopal Church Service Cross; a Protestant «world evangelization» symbol in the 20th century (Wikipedia, Jerusalem cross [verified]). **Verdict: contested** (Crusader association; adopted by some far-right groups [memory — not checked this run, flag]).

**Tau cross.** Franciscan: «Saint Francis of Assisi, who adopted it as his personal sign», now the symbol of the Secular Franciscan Order (Wikipedia, Tau cross [verified]); Tertullian reads the Tau via Ezekiel 9:4 (Wikipedia, Staurogram [verified]). **Verdict: tradition-leaning (Franciscan).**

## 4. Images, iconoclasm and what each tradition avoids

- Heidelberg Catechism, Lord's Day 35: Q96 «We are not to make an image of God in any way»; Q97 «God cannot and may not be visibly portrayed in any way»; Q98 God wants his people taught «not by means of dumb images but by the living preaching of his Word» (heidelberg-catechism.com, https://www.heidelberg-catechism.com/en/lords-days/35.html [verified]).
- «Calvinism rejected all images in churches, and this practice continues today in conservative Reformed (Calvinist) churches, as well as in Anabaptist Christianity. The Catholic Church and the Lutheran Church defend the use of sacred images» (Wikipedia, Iconoclasm [verified]).
- Orthodox do not use symbolic Lamb images in liturgical iconography (above).
- Consequence for a neutral mark [inference]: no image of Christ (icon, Lamb, Good Shepherd, staurogram read as a pictogram), no crucifix; prefer letters, scribal signs and abstract geometry, which no tradition rejects.

## 5. Early-Church symbols (pre-division, before any modern tradition)

- **Anchor**: Heb 6:19–20; «A fragment of inscription discovered in the catacomb of St. Domitilla contains the anchor; it dates from the end of the 1st century»; frequent in 2nd–3rd-century epitaphs (Wikipedia, Christian symbolism, from the Catholic Encyclopedia [verified]). **Shared**, but secular readings dominate (navy, tattoos, maritime brands) [inference].
- **Good Shepherd**: «the most common of the symbolic representations of Christ found in the Catacombs of Rome» (same [verified]). **Shared** but pictorial (illegible at 16 px; an image of Christ — §4).
- **Dove**: Holy Spirit (Mt 3:16), frequent in ancient art (same [verified]). **Shared**, the #2 church-logo cliché (§7).

## 6. Bookish and scribal signs (secular-bookish, several with patristic use)

- **Codex.** Early Christians strongly preferred the codex while rolls stayed dominant for non-Christian literature in the 2nd–3rd centuries (Hurtado, *The Earliest Christian Artifacts*, 2006, as summarised in search results; BMCR review https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/?p=25467 [snippet]); one secondary source reports 71 % of surviving 2nd-century Christian books were codices vs 4.9 % of all books (University of Richmond course blog citing Hurtado [snippet] — check before quoting a number). The codex is the one book form that is distinctively early-Christian **and** neutral across today's traditions [inference]. Risk: «open book» is the most common Bible-site cliché (02 §4) — a codex has to be drawn as a construction (quire, spine, leaf), not as an open Bible.
- **Hexapla columns.** Origen's Hexapla (before 240): six parallel versions of the Old Testament in columns, «an immense and complex word-for-word comparison» (Wikipedia, Hexapla [verified]); Origen marked the Septuagint column with Aristarchus's critical signs, the obelus and asterisk, closed by the metobelus; sources disagree on which sign marked what (Catholic Encyclopedia via catholic.org; ANF via ccel.org [snippet]). Parallel columns are the visual ancestor of comparing texts side by side. Caveat: Origen was posthumously condemned in the 6th century [memory]; the Hexapla as a tool is uncontroversial [inference].
- **Eusebian canons**: Eusebius's system of ~1165 Gospel sections and canon tables cross-referencing parallel passages, in «nearly all Greek and Latin manuscripts» from late antiquity (Wikipedia, Eusebian canons [verified]). The canon table (arcaded columns of numbers) is a patristic-era study device, owned by no modern tradition.
- **Pilcrow ¶**: from the Greek *paragraphos* (a horizontal line in the margin) and the medieval rubricated capitulum (Wikipedia, Pilcrow [verified]). **Secular-bookish**; already used by text-editing software [memory].
- **Section sign §**: references numbered sections, mostly legal codes (Wikipedia, Section sign [verified]). **Secular-legal** — reads as «law», a wrong note for doctrine [inference].
- **Manicule ☞**: Renaissance readers' pointing hand; first dedicated study William H. Sherman, «Towards a History of the Manicule» (2005) (Wikipedia, Manicule [verified]). **Secular-bookish**, but a hand (DESIGN's «no stock hands» is about photos; still a risk) and illegible at 16 px.
- **Obelus / asterisk / dagger**: Aristarchan critical signs; the obelus marked «erroneous or dubious content» (Wikipedia, Obelus [verified]). **Secular-bookish with a patristic use (Origen)**; but † reads as death/cross in modern text and DESIGN already reserves «†» for death dates.
- **Guillemets « »**: first appear in a 1527 book printed by Josse Bade (Wikipedia, Guillemet [verified]); Spanish and French quotation marks; the site uses them for quotations (DESIGN). **Secular-bookish, Spanish-native** — «each tradition speaks in its own words» is literally «in quotation marks» [inference].
- **Diptych / triptych / parallel columns**: generic; triptych has an altarpiece connotation (Western) [memory].

## 7. What secular and non-religious readers read into the signs

- **Recognition**: NN/g: «most icons continue to be ambiguous to users»; «Intricate details are difficult to distinguish at smaller sizes» (Aurora Harley, «Icon Usability», 2014-07-27, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/ [verified]). A cross is one of the few universally recognised signs; that is exactly why it brands the site as «Christian organisation» rather than «study resource» [inference].
- **Religious cues lower trust for the less religious**: Taylor, Halstead & Haynes, «Consumer Responses to Christian Religious Symbols in Advertising», *Journal of Advertising* 39(2), 2010, doi:10.2753/JOA0091-3367390206 [Crossref verified]; the finding that the Christian symbol reduced perceived service quality for consumers with weaker religious beliefs is from a search summary [snippet; abstract elided by the publisher]. Kalliny et al., *Journal of Global Marketing* 2020: attitudes to religious symbols in ads depend on the consumer's religion, country, commitment, and «an ad's level of symbolism explicitness» (Semantic Scholar abstract, doi:10.1080/08911762.2019.1669760 [verified]).
- **Church-logo clichés**: a Church Marketing Sucks reader poll on overdone church-logo symbols: the cross «57%», the dove «29%» (churchmarketingsucks.com/2006/07/church-symbols-poll [snippet]); the same site's «How to Avoid the Overused Lambs, Doves & Flames» [snippet]. Rebrands that dropped explicit symbols (Uniting Church in Australia's welfare arm, a C of E school) drew criticism from Christian press [snippet].
- One-line reading [inference from the above, not a study]: a cross or dove reads «church»; an open book reads «Bible app»; a Greek letter or scribal sign reads «scholarly / classical»; an abstract geometric mark reads «tech product». For a site whose promise is «primary sources, every tradition in its own words», the scholarly reading is the right one.
