## Method and threshold

The search began with broad Spanish queries (`debate eucaristía católico protestante`, `debate misa católico evangélico`, `Juan 6 carne nada aprovecha eucaristía debate`) and then followed the highest-view results that contained an actual objector rather than a Catholic monologue or reaction. The three highest-view relevant videos were read in full. Two lower-view independent exchanges were then read in full to test recurrence across affiliations.

An objection passes only if:

1. at least three independent people actually voice it;
2. the wording is recoverable with a timestamp and short quotation;
3. the speaker's affiliation is stated by the speaker or independently documented;
4. an allowed objecting official text supplies a genuine doctrinal anchor; and
5. an official Catholic source answers the proposition rather than a weaker substitute.

Views are snapshots from the YouTube watch-page data read on 2026-10-21, not timeless popularity claims.

## Objections heard

### EO-1 · “Si Cristo se sacrificó una vez para siempre, ¿por qué la Misa vuelve a ofrecer un sacrificio?”

1. **Neutral statement.** Hebrews presents Christ's offering as once for all. Calling every Mass a propitiatory sacrifice sounds like another offering, a repetition, or an admission that the cross was insufficient.
2. **Popular wording.** Gustavo: «La Biblia enseña claramente que fue una vez y para siempre» (D2, 14:45–15:00). Medardo: «se ofreció una vez por todas» (D4, 4:35–5:24). Harry: «solamente fue un sacrificio» (D5, 4:39–5:35). Néstor argues that if forgiveness rests on the repeated sign, salvation no longer rests on the cross (D3, 1:19:44–1:20:24).
3. **Heard from.** 4 independent people: one evangelical, one Lutheran bishop, one Pentecostal, and one Reformed speaker.
4. **Catholic answer.** The Catholic claim includes the “once for all”: Trent XXII chs. 1–2 says the victim and offerer are the same and only the manner of offering differs; CCC 1364 and 1367 call the Eucharist the memorial that makes present the one sacrifice; `Ecclesia de Eucharistia` 12 says the Mass neither adds to nor multiplies the cross. The answer must not be shortened to “it is only a memorial,” because Catholic doctrine also calls it propitiatory and applies its fruit to living and dead.
5. **Objecting official-text anchor.** Westminster Confession 29.2 explicitly opposes any real sacrifice for living or dead to Christ's one offering. The Apology of the Augsburg Confession XXIV.19, 22 distinguishes eucharistic thanksgiving from the one propitiatory sacrifice, Christ's death. Current WCF: https://opc.org/wcf.html. Book of Concord text: https://bookofconcord.org/defense/of-the-mass/.
6. **Affiliation note.** The four speakers do not mean exactly the same thing. Néstor affirms a Reformed real spiritual participation; Medardo's memorialist formulation is not the Book of Concord's Lutheran account of the Supper.
7. **Dossier overlap.** Exact overlap with `catholic.md` §4 OB-1; partial overlap with OB-5 on applications for the dead.
8. **Verdict.** **Pass.** Strong recurrence, cross-affiliation spread, exact official anchors, and an exact Catholic answer.

### EO-2 · “Es pan y vino que representan a Cristo; no su cuerpo literal ni una sustancia corporal presente”

1. **Neutral statement.** The bread and cup signify, proclaim, or spiritually mediate Christ and his sacrifice. Jesus often speaks metaphorically, so “this is my body” and “I am the bread” need not mean that the substance of bread ceases to be bread.
2. **Popular wording.** Miguel: «Yo creo que Jesucristo estaba hablando metafóricamente» (D1, 11:37–12:55). Medardo says the host «parece pan… porque es pan» (D4, 1:19–1:45). Néstor describes a «presencia espiritual de Cristo, no de su cuerpo» (D3, 39:19–41:28). Gustavo calls the Supper symbolic and reads eating as intimate communion with Christ (D2, 18:49–23:14). Harry says Jesus could not have converted the bread into his body while bodily present at the table (D5, 5:43–6:19).
3. **Heard from.** 5 independent people: Baptist attendee, evangelical, Reformed, Lutheran, and Pentecostal.
4. **Catholic answer.** Trent XIII chs. 1 and 4 and canons 1–2 distinguish sacramental presence from a mere sign and define the conversion of the whole substance of bread and wine. CCC 1374–1376 repeats “true, real and substantial” presence and calls the conversion transubstantiation. A fair answer must also concede that the outward species remain and that Catholic teaching does not claim visible flesh or ordinary spatial presence.
5. **Objecting official-text anchor.** Baptist Faith and Message 2000 VII calls the Supper a “symbolic act of obedience” that memorializes the Redeemer's death: https://bfm.sbc.net/bfm2000/#vii. Westminster 29.5 says the elements “still remain truly and only bread and wine” in substance and nature; 29.7 affirms real spiritual reception “not carnally and corporally”; 29.6 rejects transubstantiation: https://opc.org/wcf.html.
6. **Affiliation note.** This is a family of objections, not one common positive doctrine. Baptist memorialism and Reformed spiritual presence disagree with each other. The Book of Concord rejects transubstantiation but affirms the true body and blood in the Supper, so Medardo's “because it is bread” line cannot be labelled simply “the Lutheran position.”
7. **Dossier overlap.** Partial OB-2: the popular layer is broader than the official-text wording there (memorialism and Reformed spiritual presence are not in OB-2, whose anchors are SA III.6.5 and WCF 29.5–29.6).
8. **Verdict.** **Pass**, provided the site preserves the memorialist/Reformed/Lutheran distinctions instead of merging them.

### EO-3 · “Primera Corintios 11 exige respetar el significado de la Cena, no discernir una sustancia cambiada”

1. **Neutral statement.** Paul's rebuke concerns faction, gluttony, drunkenness, and failure to honour Christ's sacrifice and the gathered body. Being guilty of the body and blood need not imply that bread has become Christ's body.
2. **Popular wording.** Miguel says the Corinthians had turned the meal into a party and failed to respect its purpose (D1, 6:45–8:20). Gustavo says unworthy reception is taking lightly what the Savior's death cost (D2, 32:00–33:00). Néstor says they were «ninguneando el carácter sagrado del acto eucarístico», not proving a literal change (D3, 34:50–36:42).
3. **Heard from.** 3 independent people: Baptist attendee, evangelical, and Reformed.
4. **Catholic answer.** CCC 1385 quotes 1 Corinthians 11:27–29 as the reason for examining oneself before Communion; Trent XIII ch. 7 uses the same passage for worthy reception. The deeper Catholic argument joins vv. 27–29 to 1 Corinthians 10:16: one is guilty of the body and blood because Communion is participation in them, while Paul's social rebuke remains fully real. The answer should not deny the meal-abuse context.
5. **Objecting official-text anchor.** Westminster 29.8 says the ignorant and wicked receive the outward elements but not the thing signified, yet by unworthy reception are guilty of the body and blood. That is the closest exact confessional form of this reading: https://opc.org/wcf.html.
6. **Affiliation note.** Miguel and Gustavo offer a memorial reading; Néstor embeds the same exegesis in Reformed sacramental realism.
7. **Dossier overlap.** Supports OB-2 but is not presently a separate objection in §4. It is a recurrent exegetical sub-objection worth keeping under OB-2.
8. **Verdict.** **Pass as a sub-objection**, not as a separate top-level card unless the UI later needs a scriptural-detail layer.

### EO-4 · “El cuerpo humano de Cristo está en el cielo; una presencia corporal en muchas hostias implicaría dos cuerpos o muchas venidas”

1. **Neutral statement.** Christ's glorified human body is at the Father's right hand. Saying that the same body was at the table and in the bread, or is now bodily present in many places, appears to give the human nature omnipresence and to confuse sacramental presence with another incarnation or return.
2. **Popular wording.** Medardo says Christ «ahí está todavía» at the Father's right hand (D4, 5:43–6:19). Harry says Christ could not be present in the bread while his human body was at the table and later repeats that he is now at the Father's right hand (D5, 5:43–6:19; 17:20–18:21). Néstor develops the “two incarnations,” local body, and repeated-coming argument (D3, 45:31–50:40; 1:05:16–1:11:26).
3. **Heard from.** 3 independent people: Lutheran, Pentecostal, and Reformed.
4. **Catholic answer.** Trent XIII ch. 1 expressly holds both propositions: Christ is naturally at the Father's right hand and is sacramentally present in many places “by a manner of existing” proper to the sacrament. CCC 1374 calls this mode unique; the Roman Catechism, II.4.37, denies that the body arrives by local motion. The honest answer is “a different sacramental mode,” not a proof that Christ's human body is spatially ubiquitous.
5. **Objecting official-text anchor.** Westminster 29.7 says Christ's body and blood are received spiritually, while the elements remain outward signs; 29.4 and 29.6 reject adoration and transubstantiation. Formula of Concord, Epitome VII.32 (item 11 of the rejected Sacramentarian theses) rejects the thesis «That the body of Christ is so enclosed in heaven that it can in no way be at once and at one time in many or all places upon earth», showing that the location argument is Reformed, not generically Protestant: https://bookofconcord.org/epitome/the-lords-supper/. The text does not name Acts 3:21 (a WCF 29.6 proof text); linking the two is the dossier's inference.
6. **Affiliation note.** Medardo personally uses a localization argument that the confessional Lutheran Formula of Concord expressly disputes. His denominational identity and this doctrinal mismatch must both be visible.
7. **Dossier overlap.** OB-2; the dossier already notes Acts 3:21 must not be put in Lutheran mouths. The popular evidence shows one Lutheran bishop did use it, but that does not change the official-text asymmetry.
8. **Verdict.** **Pass**, with the confessional mismatch disclosed.
